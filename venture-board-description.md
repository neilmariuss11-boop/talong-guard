## One line

Pherospora is a pole-mounted station that turns the onion armyworm moth into the carrier of its own cure: a pheromone lure draws the moth in, a shaded sleeve dusts it with a natural insect-killing fungus, and the moth flies out alive to infect its mates, eggs and larvae. No spraying, no power, no mixing.

## The problem

Onion armyworm, called *harabas* by farmers, is the most destructive pest of Philippine onion. It caused ₱1.61 billion in losses in Nueva Ecija in 2016 and infested 12,138 hectares nationwide in 2024. That same year it did ₱30 million of damage in Occidental Mindoro, and San Jose, Magsaysay and Looc were placed under a state of calamity.

Farmers respond by spraying. In one survey, 305 of 310 did so during infestations. But the pest now resists the modern insecticides, with diamide resistance ratios above 2,000-fold on record, and outbreak-year input costs reportedly double to about ₱400,000 per hectare while still losing half the crop. The pheromone traps that government already distributes only monitor. Sprayed biocontrol fungi die in sunlight within hours.

## The solution

The Pherospora Station is a hand-built device with no moving parts:

1. A 40 cm white hood shelters a commercial sex-pheromone lure.
2. Male moths flying to the lure pass through an 80 mm window and drop into a 4-inch PVC tube.
3. Inside, a removable velvet sleeve carries 2 g of dry *Metarhizium anisopliae* spores, a fungus that occurs naturally in Philippine soils and is already used by PhilRice.
4. The moth leaves through the open bottom, alive and dusted. Males mate with about five females each, passing the spores on. Infected adults die in about four days, infected females lay fewer viable eggs, and spores that reach larvae kill them within about a week.

The farmer swaps the sleeve every two weeks and the lure every four. The fungus sits in the dark, protected from the ultraviolet light that kills sprayed spores.

## Why this works

Every component is published and proven. The lure is already used by the Department of Agriculture in onion fields. Local fungal isolates kill harabas. Moth-to-moth transmission has been demonstrated in the same genus. Commercial pheromone-powder stations have worked at scale in European orchards for years. What nobody sells, in the Philippines or elsewhere, is this combination for onion armyworm. Our business is to assemble the proven parts into a product and demonstrate it in real fields. We do not run hypothesis research.

## Product and business model

| Item | Price | Cost | Cycle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Station (hardware) | ₱690 | ₱285 | Sold once |
| Season Kit (3 lures + 6 spore cartridges) | ₱520 | ₱330 | Every onion season |

At four Stations per hectare a farmer pays ₱4,840 in the first season and ₱2,080 per season after that, about 0.8 to 1.8 percent of onion gross value. Preventing one percent of yield loss, or skipping one insecticide application, pays for the kit. In outbreak years it is insurance against a ₱400,000 hectare.

First customers are the ones who already buy pheromone traps: the San Jose Municipal Agriculture Office, the Provincial Agriculturist, and DA-MIMAROPA crop protection programs. Irrigators' associations and commercial growers follow.

## Market

Occidental Mindoro planted 8,637 hectares of onion in 2026, up 44 percent in one year and the fastest-growing onion area in the country. San Jose alone is about 3,285 hectares. With Nueva Ecija and other provinces the addressable market exceeds 20,000 hectares, or about 80,000 Stations and ₱41 million of kit sales per season. Our Year-3 target is about 7 percent of Occidental Mindoro.

## Regulatory pathway

The hardware is not a pesticide. The spore cartridge is a microbial pesticide under PD 1144 and needs Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority cover. We stage it: demonstration with no cartridge sales under a partner institution, then cartridges through a registered source or a government program, then our own biorational registration in Year 3.

## Plan for this school year

- Build 20 to 40 Stations from a costed bill of materials and fabrication jigs.
- Interview at least 20 onion farmers and two LGU or DA offices in San Jose.
- Run a field demonstration on 3 to 5 farms during the November 2026 to March 2027 season, with a farmer field day at the peak moth window.
- Measure what a buyer will ask about: mycosed larvae in the field, spray counts against neighbouring farms, and yield records.

## Key risks, stated plainly

- The effect is slow and works over generations, so a farmer cannot see it the next morning. We position Pherospora as prevention and fewer sprays, never as a spray replacement, and we show mycosed larvae at the field day.
- No published field trial exists for fungal autodissemination on this species. That gap is the market opportunity and the reason the demonstration comes first.
- Spore viability depends on humidity and falls with storage time, so cartridges are produced per season and kept sealed and cold.
- Fungal autodissemination has reached the market for mosquitoes and pheromone-powder stations have for orchard moths, but no company has yet made it pay for a crop moth. The reasons are registration cost and slow visible effect, not the science. Our answer is a low-cost hand-built device, a government first buyer, and a pest that has already defeated the chemicals.

## Impact

Protects a ₱200,000 to ₱300,000 per hectare farmer investment, cuts insecticide handling and residues, confines the fungus to a point source of grams per hectare, and adds a non-chemical mode of action that slows resistance. Fabrication and spore production stay local. Aligned with SDGs 1, 2, 3, 8, 12 and 15.
