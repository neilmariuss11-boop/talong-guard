## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

**Problem.** Onion armyworm (*Spodoptera exigua*, locally **harabas**) is the most destructive pest of Philippine onion:
- **2016:** it caused **₱1.61 billion** in losses in Nueva Ecija [R12].
- **2024:** it infested **12,138 hectares** of onion nationwide [R16].
- **2024, Occidental Mindoro:** ₱30 M in damage, with San Jose, Magsaysay and Looc under a state of calamity [R17].

Occidental Mindoro now plants **8,637 ha** of onion (2026), the fastest-growing onion area in the country [R18]. Farmers respond by spraying. 305 of 310 surveyed farmers spray during infestations [R21b]. But harabas resists modern insecticides [R43], and outbreak-year input costs reportedly double to about ₱400,000/ha [R21].

**Solution.** The Station is a pole-mounted, solar-free, battery-free device that works in four steps:
1. A **sex-pheromone lure** draws male harabas moths at night.
2. On the way to the lure, each moth brushes against a **velvet sleeve dusted with dry *Metarhizium anisopliae* spores**, a naturally occurring insect-killing fungus.
3. The moth **flies away alive**.
4. When it mates (males mate with about 5 females each [R33]), it passes spores to females. Infected moths die in about 4 days [R4], infected females lay fewer and less viable eggs [R4], and spores reaching larvae kill them within about a week [R1, R10].

The farmer doesn't mix or spray anything. The fungus sits in the dark inside the device, protected from the sunlight that normally kills sprayed spores within hours [R25].

**Why now:**
- Every component is published and proven.
  - The lure is already used by DA in onion fields [R13, R16, R22].
  - Local isolates of the fungus kill harabas [R1, R15].
  - Moth-to-moth transmission is shown in the same genus [R4].
  - Commercial pheromone-powder stations work at scale in Europe [R32].
- **Nobody sells this combination** anywhere we could find, least of all in the Philippines.

**Business model:**
- The **Station** is sold once (₱690).
- A **Season Kit** is sold each onion season (₱520 per Station: 3 lures + 6 spore cartridges).
- At 4 Stations/ha, a farmer pays **₱4,840/ha in the first season and ₱2,080/ha after**. That is **0.8–1.8% of onion gross value** (9 t/ha × ₱30/kg ≈ ₱271,800/ha). Preventing **one percent** of yield loss pays for the kit.

**First customers:**
- (1) Local government units (LGUs) and DA-MIMAROPA crop protection programs, which **already buy pheromone traps** [R22].
- (2) Irrigators' associations and farmer cooperatives in San Jose and Magsaysay.
- (3) Individual commercial onion growers.

**Traction plan (this school year):**
- Build 20–40 Stations.
- Interview 20+ farmers and 2 LGU/DA offices.
- Run a field demonstration in San Jose during the **Nov 2026–Mar 2027** onion season.

**Financials (base case, `phase7-financial-model.md`):**
- Year 1 pilot: ₱41,400 revenue (Station hardware only; no cartridges are sold during the demonstration season), a ₱170,100 loss.
- Year 2: ₱0.77 M revenue, close to break-even.
- Year 3: ₱2.70 M revenue and ₱393,360 net income, cumulative positive (+₱175,460) by Year 3.

Biggest sensitivity: **lure landed cost**. The model needs ≤₱60 per lure, so we must source from Indian or Chinese suppliers, not US retail.

**Ask:** approval of the concept and pilot, adviser support for a partnership letter to PhilRice, NCPC-UPLB or DA-RCPC (starter culture and legal cover), and **₱180,000** in Year-1 pilot funding (class funds, MinSU, or competitions), with a TECHNiCOM application after the pilot.

## 2. THE PROBLEM

### 2.1 The pest
- Harabas larvae defoliate onion. Outbreaks go from manageable to catastrophic within days [R12, R21].
- Adults are nocturnal moths. Males find females by following her sex pheromone, and each male mates several times [R33].
- Trap catches in PH onion **peak 25–59 days after planting** [R13]. That is the window when protection matters most.
- Outbreaks can **arrive by migration**, not just build locally. The 2016 outbreak was traced to mass long-distance migration, possibly El Niño-driven [R12].

### 2.2 The damage (hard numbers)

| Event | Scale | Loss |
|---|---|---|
| Nueva Ecija 2016 | 5,330 ha, 4,089 farmers, 14 towns | ₱1,609,997,316 [R12] |
| National, May 2024 | 12,137.92 ha infested; 674 ha total loss; 4,045 ha partial | [R16] |
| MIMAROPA, 2024 | 871.6 ha partially damaged | [R16] |
| Occidental Mindoro, Mar 2024 | San Jose, Magsaysay, Looc under state of calamity | ₱30 M armyworm damage [R17] |
| Bongabon farmer testimony | "Infestation approximately every three years since 2016"; ₱600,000 debts; one farmer suicide reported | [R21a] |
| Pangasinan outbreak | ~₱400,000/ha inputs vs ~₱200,000 normal, saving only about half the crop | [R21] |

### 2.3 Why current solutions fail

| Current practice | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Spraying insecticides (near-universal) [R21b] | Resistance: diamide (chlorantraniliprole) resistance ratios up to 2,477-fold; emamectin resistance documented [R43]. Cost spikes in outbreak years. Health and residue concerns |
| Pheromone traps (DA-distributed) [R16, R22] | They **only monitor**. They tell the farmer when to spray, which saves sprays [R13, R14], but they don't reduce the moth population |
| Spraying *Metarhizium* [R11, R15] | Works in principle, but sunlight kills sprayed spores within hours [R25]. Needs repeated late-afternoon application and mixing |
| Leaving onion farming | Bongabon onion area fell from 2,800 to 1,800 ha due to investment risk [R21b] |

### 2.4 The insight
> **The pheromone trap is already in the field. It just catches the wrong thing.** The trap catches moths it can count. Instead, it should **arm** them with a disease they carry back to the population. The male moth becomes the delivery system, and he goes exactly where the females are.

## 3. THE SOLUTION: HOW THE STATION WORKS

### 3.1 In one picture

```
                 ┌──────────── 40 cm white rain/sun hood (opaque) ────────────┐
                 │                   [ LURE CAGE ]  ← pheromone rubber septum │
                 │                          │         (height adjustable)     │
                 └──────────────────┬───────┴───────┬─────────────────────────┘
    moth flies →        3 rods      │ baffle vanes  │  ← 8 cm open ENTRY WINDOW (360°)
    upwind to the lure              │ (moth hits,   │     smooth cross-vanes: moth hits, drops
                                    │  drops)       │
                                    ├───────────────┤
                                    │ ▒ 5 cm sun  ▒ │ ← plain "sun collar" (no spores)
                                    │ ▒ collar    ▒ │
                                    │ ░░░░░░░░░░░░░ │ ← 4-inch PVC tube, 20 cm total,
                                    │ ░ velvet  ░░░ │   lined with removable
                                    │ ░ sleeve  ░░░ │   VELVET SPORE CARTRIDGE (15 cm)
                                    │ ░ + 2 g   ░░░ │   (2 g dry M. anisopliae)
                                    │ ░ spores  ░░░ │
                                    └───────┬───────┘
                                     OPEN BOTTOM → moth exits alive, dusted
                                            │
                                     bamboo / PVC pole; tube bottom ~0.6 m above soil

   Lure-to-velvet distance ≥ 21 cm. Sun can reach the velvet only when the sun is below ~29° elevation.
```

### 3.2 Sequence (one night)
1. **Dusk.** The lure's plume spreads downwind across the onion field.
2. **Night (second half, peak mating [R33]).** Male moths fly upwind to the Station, circle the lure, hit the baffle vanes (the same mechanism as the common "unitrap"), and drop into the velvet-lined tube.
3. **Contact.** Fluttering and crawling down the tube, the moth's legs, body and wings pick up spores. Velvet holds spores well and releases them on contact [R6].
4. **Exit.** The tube bottom is open. There is no funnel, no glue and no poison, so the moth leaves and keeps searching for females.
5. **Next 1–4 days.** The moth mates. Spores pass to the female [R4]. Both moths become infected. Moths still carry high spore numbers 72 h after contamination [R4].
6. **Days 3–7.** Infected adults die (LT50 ≈ 4 days) [R4]. Infected females lay fewer eggs, and fewer of those eggs hatch [R4]. Some isolates kill 80–87% of eggs directly [R39].
7. **Following week.** Larvae that do hatch meet spores on eggs, leaves and cadavers. *Metarhizium* kills harabas larvae in about 3–7 days in lab studies [R1, R10]. Dead insects grow new green spores, which starts a **second round** of infection [R11].

### 3.3 What the farmer does
- **Install:** hammer the pole in at transplanting, clip the Station on, snap in the lure, slide in a cartridge. About 5 minutes per Station.
- **Every 2 weeks:** swap the spore cartridge (sealed foil pouch → slide out the old sleeve → slide in the new one).
- **Every 4 weeks:** swap the lure.
- **End of season:** take the Station home, clean it, store it for next season.

No mixing, no spraying, no electricity, no smartphone.

## 4. WHY THIS WORKS: THE EVIDENCE CHAIN

Each link is published. None needs original research by us.

| Link | Claim | Evidence | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Harabas males come to the lure in PH onion | Arida et al. 2003, 2004 (PH onion) [R13, R14]; DA uses these lures now [R16, R22] | HIGH |
| 2 | Moths pick up dry spores from a velvet device | Velvet autoinoculation devices [R5, R6]; commercial powder pickup at pheromone stations (Exosex) [R32] | HIGH (other species) |
| 3 | Moth pheromones don't block the fungus | FAW lure: no effect on germination [R4]; FCM pheromone: no effect [R7] | HIGH |
| 4 | Spore-carrying *Spodoptera* moths die and infect mates | *M. anisopliae* ICIPE 7: 100% FAW moth mortality, LT50 3.9 d; moth-to-moth transmission; reduced egg-laying and hatch [R4] | HIGH (same genus) |
| 5 | The fungus kills harabas larvae | *M. rileyi* PH isolate 100% at 10⁷–10⁸ conidia/mL, 7 d [R1]; *M. anisopliae* FT83 100% at 10⁷ conidia/mL, 3 d [R10]; OMSC San Jose onion field trial [R15] | HIGH |
| 6 | The concept works beyond the lab | Diamondback moth field-cage epizootics [R8]; thrips field device [R5]; FCM multi-location orchard trial [R7] | MEDIUM |
| **Gap** | **The whole chain on harabas in a PH onion field** | **Not yet published anywhere** | This is the **market gap** we fill, and what our field *demonstration* shows |

**Why *M. anisopliae* and not *M. rileyi*.** Our earlier notes preferred *M. rileyi*. The evidence changes that choice (`research-synthesis.md` §3):
- *M. rileyi* has no published adult-moth efficacy.
- It has no ovicidal activity [R3].
- It is hard to mass-produce [R34].
- *M. anisopliae* has adult-moth data [R4], egg data [R39], a PhilRice farmer-level production protocol [R11], and local harabas data [R15].

*M. rileyi* stays on the roadmap as a v2 cartridge upgrade with NCPC-UPLB.

"TalongGuard" means eggplant guard and no longer fits. Criteria: easy for farmers to say, meaningful in Filipino, not tied to one crop forever, trademark-searchable at IPOPHL.

|---|---|---|---|
| **Padala** | "Something sent/delivered" (as in a *padala* from abroad) | Tells the story: **the moth delivers the cure to its own kind**; warm and familiar; crop-neutral | Common word, so a trademark may need a mark like "Padala Trap" |

## 19. PANEL Q&A PREPARATION (be over-prepared)

**Q1. "Isn't this just a pheromone trap?"**
No. A trap *removes* moths. It catches a few and ignores the rest, and it doesn't reduce the next generation. The Station *arms* each visiting male with a fungus he carries to about 5 females [R33], so each visit reaches many more insects than the one moth. The pheromone is the delivery address; the fungus is the package.

**Q2. "You said no research. Isn't testing this research?"**
Every biological claim is already published (§4). What we do is **product validation**: does *our* device pick up spores, keep them alive and satisfy farmers? That is quality testing and customer discovery, the same thing any product company does before launch.

**Q3. "Where's the proof it works in the field for harabas?"**
The chain is proven link by link:
- Lure attraction in PH onion [R13, R14].
- Spore pickup from velvet devices [R5, R6].
- Pheromone compatibility [R4, R7].
- Moth death and moth-to-moth transmission in the same genus [R4].
- Larval kill with PH isolates [R1].
- Field-scale autodissemination in other moths [R7, R8].

The **full combination on harabas has not been published**, which is exactly why no one sells it. Our demo will produce the first local field evidence as part of normal product validation.

**Q4. "Why *M. anisopliae* and not *M. rileyi*? NCPC says *M. rileyi* gives 100% mortality."**
*M. rileyi* gives 100% mortality to **larvae** in the lab [R1]. Our device contaminates **adult moths**, and adult data exist only for *M. anisopliae* [R4]. *M. rileyi* is also hard to mass-produce and short-lived [R34], and it showed no egg-killing [R3]. We'll add it later with NCPC.

**Q5. "Won't the pheromone kill the fungus?"**
Studies with moth sex pheromones (fall armyworm, false codling moth) found **no inhibition** [R4, R7]. The inhibition reported elsewhere was from a thrips lure and a whitefly plant volatile [R5, R6]. By design the lure still sits at least 21 cm from the spores, as free insurance.

**Q6. "Sunlight kills *Metarhizium*."**
Correct. UV-B kills half of the conidia in about 4.4 hours [R25]. That's why sprayed *Metarhizium* fails and why our spores sit inside an opaque tube under a hood. This is one of our main advantages over spraying.

**Q7. "How long do the spores last?"**
In storage at 25 °C, about 72% remain viable after 4.5 months [R26]. So we produce per season and keep cartridges sealed with silica gel, refrigerated. In the device, about 2 weeks (≥86% at 12 days in a similar device [R5]). Hence the 14-day swap.

**Q8. "Is it safe for people, bees and animals?"**
The US-EPA concluded "reasonable certainty of no harm" for *M. anisopliae*; it is not harmful to honey bees and has low toxicity to lady beetles and lacewings [R28]. Farmers never touch loose powder: sleeves come sealed, and gloves are included.

**Q9. "Isn't selling spores illegal without FPA registration?"**
Yes. Selling a microbial pesticide requires FPA registration or a permit [R29]. That's why v1 sells the **hardware and lures**, runs the demo under a partner/EUP, and fills cartridges from a registered source or a government program until our own biorational registration (§9.2).

**Q10. "Will farmers pay when onion is only ₱30/kg?"**
At ₱30/kg a hectare still grosses about ₱271,800 [R20, R36]. The kit is 0.77% of that. We also sell through LGUs and associations, which already buy pheromone traps [R22].

**Q11. "How many Stations per hectare, and why?"**
We start at 4/ha (about 50 m spacing). Monitoring uses 8–10 traps/ha and mass trapping 40–50/ha for a related armyworm [R23b]. Autodissemination needs fewer because moths spread the fungus themselves, but density matters [R48]. The demo compares 2 vs 4/ha.

**Q12. "What if the moths just die inside?"**
There's no kill agent or glue. The fungus takes days to kill (LT50 ≈ 4 days [R4]), so the moth leaves healthy-looking and mates first.

**Q13. "What about other pests like thrips?"**
The Station targets harabas only; thrips need a different approach. Swappable lures for *S. litura* and fall armyworm are on the v2 roadmap using the same body.

**Q14. "What's novel here?"**
- (1) The **first pheromone-baited *Metarhizium* autodissemination product for *Spodoptera exigua*** that we could find.
- (2) A **noctuid-scale device design** (baffle vanes + open velvet tube + separated lure hood) adapted from thrips and whitefly devices [R5, R6].
- (3) A **locally produced cartridge supply chain** based on PhilRice's protocol [R11].
- (4) A **B2G + cooperative go-to-market** built on an existing DA habit of buying pheromone traps [R22].

**Q15. "Can someone copy it?"**
The concept is prior art (an expired 1990s US patent [R42]). Our moat is execution: a Utility Model on our specific design [R30], DA/LGU relationships, cartridge logistics and the first local field data.

**Q16. "What if it rains?"**
The 40 cm hood shelters the tube. Onion is a dry-season crop (Nov–Apr) [R20b]. Sleeves are replaced every 2 weeks anyway.

**Q17. "How is this different from the OMSC Metarhizium study in San Jose?"**
OMSC **sprayed** liquid *M. anisopliae* on onion [R15]. That proves the fungus works locally against harabas, but spraying requires repeated labor and exposes spores to sunlight. We deliver the same fungus with **moths as the sprayer**, and in the dark.

**Q18. "Why should the LGU buy this instead of just giving pesticides?"**
It's cheaper per hectare than emergency spraying, it's non-chemical (aligned with DA's biocontrol direction [R16]), and it produces visible field-day results for the program.
